Seenchan Game · PC · Steam · Solo Project

BAL Solid

System Technical Level

A relaxing physics ball puzzle I designed, built, and shipped to Steam completely alone in ~110 hours. The mission I gave myself was simple: stop prototyping forever and actually finish and release a game — which meant every system had to be lean enough for one person, but deep enough to carry 70 levels.

Key Design Decisions
One flexible block, hundreds of puzzles
Problem I'm a one-man team — there was no way I could afford building tons of different object types.
Choice So I made one block that can do everything — resize, get destroyed, rotate, move, blow wind, or kill the ball on touch. Just toggle what you need.
Result That single object became the building block of the entire game. Minimal tech cost, basically unlimited puzzle variety.
Physics: a double-edged sword
Problem Physics gives you those "whoa" moments and lets players express themselves — but it also makes results inconsistent and hard to repeat. Classic double-edged sword.
Choice I leaned into the fun side, then covered the sharp edge: every level must be solvable in multiple ways.
Result Instead of a broken puzzle when physics misbehaves, players just discover a different solve each run. The flaw became the feature.
My 4-rule pacing system
Problem 70 levels in a row can easily turn into a repetitive grind — or worse, a difficulty wall.
Choice I sorted every level into 4 types — Simple, Reflex, Trial-and-error, Complex — then interleaved them so a tough puzzle is always followed by a breather.
Result A rhythm that keeps players challenged without burning them out — and a reusable rule I can apply to any future content.
Achievements for every player type
Problem Solving puzzles only really rewards the Explorer type. What about everyone else?
Choice I added an achievement list with Steam notifications and alphanumeric achievement icons — bait for the Achievers, plus a bit of show-off material for the Fighter/Socialiser types.
Result A deliberately niche reward structure for a niche market — and it landed: 100% positive reviews on release.

Agate International · UE4 · PC / Console

Valthirian Arc: Hero School Story 2

System Level UX 3C

Run your own hero school: manage faculty, raise a roster of students, and climb the ranks of the world's academies. This was my home for three years at Agate — I started as Senior Designer and finished as Lead, owning the economy, the proficiency & battle-stats systems, UX, and 3C across both base-building and battle.

Key Design Decisions
An Excel simulator for economy balance
Problem Balancing a two-resource economy by playtesting alone is painfully slow — and you're mostly guessing.
Choice I built an Excel simulator that mirrors the in-game economy, so I could predict and tune the balance before anything got implemented.
Result Data-backed balancing decisions and far fewer expensive in-engine iterations.
A monster placement tool for the team
Problem Placing and editing monsters was quietly eating my level designers' time.
Choice I designed an improved placement system that made the whole job fast and painless.
Result Cut designer workload on that task by ~60%. The team got their hours back.
Systems legible to players and designers
Problem The proficiency & battle-stats systems confused players — and honestly, they weren't much fun for designers to tweak either.
Choice I reworked them to be clearer for players and easier to modify for us, and rebuilt the FTUE highlight system so designers control it directly.
Result Player surveys validated the UX improvements, and the FTUE changes raised retention.

UE4 · Horror Survival · Solo Game Jam

Howling Dark

Level Technical

A narrative survival horror set in space — picture The Forest, but in pitch black. I built it solo in one week for an internal Gamecan game jam, which meant ruthless prioritization: if a system couldn't be made reusable and data-driven, it didn't get made.

Systems Built in 10 Days
Mining with a randomized spawner
Problem Resource gathering is the core of survival — but hand-placing resources is slow, and makes every run identical.
Choice I built the mining system around a randomized spawner instead.
Result Way less implementation time, and every run feels different. Two birds, one stone.
Data-driven crafting
Problem In a one-week jam, crafting recipes need rebalancing constantly — I couldn't afford to touch code every time.
Choice I backed crafting with an easily editable database.
Result Recipes and costs tunable in seconds, zero code changes.
Costly combat against the Specter
Problem A horror monster has to stay scary — give players too much firepower and it becomes a shooting gallery.
Choice I let the player fight back, but made combat expensive in resources.
Result Every encounter becomes a question: fight or conserve? That tension is the survival loop.
Needs that drive the night loop
Problem Survival pressure needs a source that never stops ticking.
Choice I added oxygen and electricity needs — plus an openable helm for risk-takers — that bite hardest during the long night.
Result A constant survival clock that forces players to weigh every resource and every risk.

UE5 · Capture the Flag · Level Study

Alaskan Oil Rig — CTF Map

Level Combat

A 6v6 Capture-the-Flag map on a fully-automated cyberpunk oil rig off the Alaskan coast, built to train my FPS level design and learn UE5. Symmetrical layout for fairness, a giant crane as the focal point (visible from at least 70% of all walkable paths — that was a hard rule), 3 main choke points, and a flying cargo robot that occasionally drops an explosive barrel just to keep everyone honest.

Key Design Decisions
The map I scrapped entirely
Problem My first blockout — an eco-futuristic Bali resort — tested terribly. Players said it felt nothing like an Overwatch team battle. More like a CS deathmatch map.
Choice I scrapped the whole idea and rebuilt from the feedback and new references — then rebalanced the weapons and AI to match the team-battle feel.
Result Same playtesters, second map: positive reactions. The do-over taught me more than the first draft ever could.
Balanced for all engagement ranges
Problem A CTF map skewed to one range favors specific heroes and feels unfair fast.
Choice I designed medium choke points for team fights, corners for surprise plays, vantage points for fliers and high-jumpers, and tuned engagement ranges (25–35 units) against real hero metrics.
Result Short, medium, and long-range heroes all get their moments.
Every area earns its place
Problem Linear CTF maps turn into one predictable contested lane — easy to turtle, boring to attack.
Choice I wrote explicit rules and purposes for all 11 areas — spawn, flag, dual corridor, flank path, long path, crane — each with defined connections and counterplay, including special paths only reachable with hero skills.
Result Attackers always have a flank option; defenders always have a fallback. No dead space.
Verticality around a focal point
Problem In a fast 6v6, players who can't orient themselves instantly are dead players.
Choice I built two height layers around the central crane as a constant visual anchor, backed by visual-aid rules: path textures must contrast with non-path objects everywhere.
Result Players always know where they are — plus risk/reward verticality for heroes who can reach it.

Genshin Impact · Custom Dungeon · Level Study

Divine Ingenuity Dungeon

Level Player Guidance

When Genshin ran its player-made dungeon event, I used it as a free level design gym: a two-room, coin-collecting, time-limited dungeon built around one question — can I guide players through a space using only the environment, zero UI markers?

Player-Guidance Techniques
Leading with positive reinforcement
Problem Players need to find the intended path without explicit waypoints.
Choice Placed collectible coins along the correct route — and deliberately none where players shouldn't go.
Result Players self-guide toward the goal by chasing rewards.
Funnel before reveal
Problem Players dropped into a space without orientation feel lost.
Choice Used a funnel up a stairway to a vantage point that reveals the whole room — goal, layout, and recovery options.
Result Players understand where they are and where to go before committing.
Teasing with incompleteness
Problem Players need motivation to explore the second room.
Choice Showed an effect missing its source (butterflies with no Electrogranum), prompting players to seek the missing piece.
Result Curiosity-driven exploration — players connect the dots themselves.
A refresher beat between challenges
Problem A timed, demanding dungeon can exhaust players.
Choice Added a speed-up zone with easy, satisfying coin gathering that doubles as required progress.
Result A fun palate-cleanser that resets focus — two birds, one stone.
UE4 · Educational Simulation · Entry Test (3 days)

Photosynthesis

System Balancing

A gamified simulation that teaches high-school students how photosynthesis actually works — built in 3 days as a company entry test. The fun part: photosynthesis is a resource-management system if you squint, so the science and the game design kept feeding each other.

Key Design Decisions
Resource management as the core metaphor
Problem The game must teach photosynthesis accurately while still playing like a game.
Choice Made the core loop balancing resources inside an enclosed space — since photosynthesis is itself energy transformation and resource balance.
Result A mechanic that's both an accurate representation and a genuine balancing challenge.
Trading some accuracy for reward
Problem Scientifically-accurate gradual growth didn't reward the player enough.
Choice Let players trigger growth on demand when sugar is sufficient — then surfaced the real biology in-context to preserve learning.
Result A deliberate, transparent fun-vs-accuracy tradeoff that keeps both.
External factors as depth
Problem A single resource loop risks feeling shallow.
Choice Turned real variables — light intensity, temperature, water — into mechanics with covers and inputs the player manages.
Result More gameplay depth and more realistic educational value at once.
Quizzes that are mechanically essential
Problem Educational quizzes are usually skippable filler.
Choice Made correct answers grant essential resources — so failing to understand the science means failing the game.
Result Learning is enforced by the core loop, directly serving the game's pillars.

In-House Engine · Java · Gameloft · First Project

N.O.V.A. Legacy — Java Port

UX Level Optimization

My very first project as a game designer — and it threw me straight into the deep end: porting a finished mobile shooter from a keypad device to the touchscreen Nokia 5800. Full control redesign, levels reworked for a new aspect ratio, brand-new content — all squeezed into a brutal ~1MB size budget. A lesson in optimization I never forgot.

Key Design Decisions
Touch controls tuned for long sessions
Problem A platform shooter built for a keypad had to become comfortable and precise on touch.
Choice Placed a fixed, transparent D-pad and buttons for muscle memory and screen visibility, and disabled slide input to suit the platforming.
Result Controls that don't tire the hands and preserve the original game's feel.
Readable QTE prompts
Problem Players must instantly recognize a quick-time event and what to press.
Choice Centered the QTE prompt, blinked the physical button, and matched the icon to that button.
Result Unmissable, unambiguous QTE moments.
New levels, narrative-accurate
Problem A more capable device meant adding levels that still fit the story and the engine's limits.
Choice Built campaign levels following the master version's narrative, plus bullet-hell levels using "bullets on screen = difficulty."
Result New content that's both story-consistent and fun within constraints.
Designing under a ~1MB budget
Problem New UI, levels, and cinematics all had to fit a tiny file-size limit.
Choice Aggressively reused and smartly split art assets across the game to add content without bloating size.
Result Shipped on time within budget — and a rare, lasting lesson in optimization.
